# Wei-Zhu Six-Element Business Model (魏朱商业模式六要素模型)

Proposed by Wei Wei (魏炜) of Peking University and Zhu Wuxiang (朱武祥) of Tsinghua University. Core definition: **a business model is the transaction structure of stakeholders.**

The six elements interact: positioning is the starting point, the business system is the core, key resources and capabilities are the support, the profit model and cash flow structure determine how and when returns arrive, and enterprise value is the final outcome.

```
          Positioning
              │
   ┌──────────┼──────────┐
Business System ── Key Resources & Capabilities
   │                     │
Profit Model ── Cash Flow Structure
              │
       Enterprise Value
```

---

## 1. Positioning

The way an enterprise meets stakeholders' needs (not just "what to sell to whom").

Questions to analyze:
- For which stakeholders (not only end customers) does it create value?
- Does it offer a product, a service, a solution, or a platform/matchmaking?
- Transaction method: one-time purchase, subscription, rental, pay-for-performance?
- Compared with competitors, are the transaction costs of this positioning (search, negotiation, fulfillment) lower?

## 2. Business system

Which actors the enterprise chooses as internal or external stakeholders, and the transaction relationships among them.

Questions to analyze:
- Configuration: Who are the stakeholders (customers, suppliers, channels, partners, employees, investors, government)? What role does each play?
- Roles: Which steps are done in-house, and which are outsourced / done through partnerships / platformized?
- Relationships: What is exchanged among the parties (products, funds, information, rights) and how is it governed (equity, contracts, revenue sharing)?
- **Draw a transaction structure diagram**: nodes = stakeholders, arrows = product/service flow, fund flow, information flow

## 3. Key resources and capabilities

The core resources and capabilities, hard to imitate, needed to make the business system run.

Questions to analyze:
- Resources: capital, licenses, brand, data, user scale, supply chain, patents, channel network
- Capabilities: operational capability, technical capability, organizational capability, integration and coordination capability
- Which **must be owned**, and which can be obtained from stakeholders?
- What is the cost and time for competitors to acquire the same resources and capabilities?

## 4. Profit model

The revenue structure, cost structure, and corresponding payment methods, divided by stakeholder.

Questions to analyze:
- Revenue sources: Who pays? (direct users, third-party payers such as advertisers, partners)
- Pricing method: fixed, residual (profit sharing), revenue share, time-based, usage-based, value/performance-based, combined pricing
- Cost structure: fixed vs variable; which costs can be shifted to stakeholders?
- Whether structures such as "free + premium", "cross-subsidy", or "third-party pays" exist
- How gross margin and marginal cost change with scale

## 5. Cash flow structure

The distribution over time of cash income net of cash investment during the enterprise's operations.

Questions to analyze:
- Is payment collected in advance (membership, stored value, deposits), i.e. a negative working capital model?
- Payment term structure: length of receivable and payable terms
- Asset weight: how much upfront capital expenditure is required?
- Stability and predictability of cash flow (subscription vs project-based)
- With the same profit, different cash flow structures bring different financing needs and risks

## 6. Enterprise value

The investment value of the enterprise, i.e. the discounted value of the free cash flow it is expected to generate in the future.

Questions to analyze:
- Growth potential: market ceiling and scalability
- Growth capability: whether the business system is replicable and whether marginal cost declines
- Efficiency: asset turnover, revenue per employee
- Risk: uncertainty of cash flow
- The business model determines the "multiple" of enterprise value: with the same revenue, valuations of a platform model vs a project-based model differ enormously

## Quick reference: classic cases

- **Focus Media (分众传媒)**: Positioning: reach mainstream urban populations for brand advertisers in "waiting scenarios" such as elevators; business system: partner with property managers to obtain placement sites; profit: charge advertisers, share revenue with property managers; key resource: scale of the site network.
- **Costco**: The core of its profit model is membership fees (collected in advance, giving a high-quality cash flow structure); goods are sold at near-zero gross margin, locking in membership renewals through low prices.
- **Nespresso**: Low-priced coffee machines, with ongoing revenue from capsules (the razor-and-blades model); patents and channels form its key resources and capabilities.
